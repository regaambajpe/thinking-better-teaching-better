01 / The idea

A better teaching decision begins with a better question.

How can I make—and evaluate—a better teaching decision when pupils’ learning, mathematical evidence and classroom conditions are interconnected?

The course develops teachers’ ability to make well-founded instructional decisions: understand pupils’ thinking, represent problems clearly, interpret evidence, recognise contextual influences and select an appropriate response.

Participants follow one recurring inquiry cycle: notice → represent → investigate → examine the system → justify a decision → evaluate and revise.

This is a proposed synthesis of the sources, not a named framework attributed to OpenLearn. It connects:

  • Reflection and action planning from Succeed with learning.
  • Mathematical modelling and interpretation from Succeed with maths.
  • Representations, misconceptions and statistical inquiry from Teaching mathematics.
  • Measurement and sampling from Mathematics for science and technology.
  • Boundaries, interrelationships and perspectives from Mastering systems thinking in practice.
  • Source evaluation, assumptions and argument mapping from Digital thinking tools for better decision making.

One case. Six connected perspectives.

They can calculate. But can they explain?

A fictional middle-school class can often calculate correctly but struggles to explain proportional relationships and evaluate numerical claims. The teacher must decide how to improve pupils’ reasoning and judge whether the change helps.

Participants work with a supplied case dossier containing fictional pupil responses, a small synthetic dataset, contrasting stakeholder perspectives and competing instructional proposals. They progressively adapt the inquiry to their own context.

Coherence by design

The same problem recurs, each module revises an earlier judgement, and every module output becomes part of the final portfolio.

Evidence-informed practice

OpenLearn supplies the conceptual backbone. EEF guidance adds classroom-facing evidence about teaching metacognitive strategies and mathematical reasoning; the National Academies adds a broader research account of learning.

The course distinguishes research findings, instructional suggestions, mathematical conclusions and locally testable hypotheses. EEF emphasises embedding metacognition within subject teaching, which supports this integrated design. Read the EEF guidance.

Who is this course for?

The primary audience is middle-school mathematics teachers, generalist teachers and teachers of science, geography or other subjects using quantitative evidence. Mathematical examples centre on proportional reasoning, percentages, elementary algebra and descriptive statistics.

Prerequisites

Participants should be able to:

  • Perform basic arithmetic and interpret straightforward tables and graphs.
  • Identify a curriculum objective and describe a classroom learning difficulty.
  • Navigate websites and save a document or image.

No coding, calculus, advanced statistics or previous systems-thinking study is required.

An opening diagnostic identifies gaps in mathematical confidence and digital familiarity. Optional refresher sections support these gaps; substantial remedial study is additional to the 30-hour course.

The default route requires neither access to a current class nor another participant. Teachers may use existing, appropriately anonymised classroom evidence instead of the supplied case. A live classroom trial is an optional follow-on.

02 / The learning journey

Six modules.
One connected course.

Move from noticing pupils’ thinking to designing an inquiry you can use. Each module adds a new perspective—and a practical piece of the final portfolio.

01Notice learning and frame an inquiryWhat do I actually know about pupils’ thinking?4 hours

Learning outcomes

Distinguish observation from interpretation; explain how planning, monitoring and evaluation can support subject learning; and formulate a manageable question about pupils’ reasoning.

Self-paced activities

  1. Complete an ungraded diagnostic involving proportional reasoning, a numerical claim and an instructional judgement.
  2. Solve a short problem while recording decisions, uncertainty and checking strategies.
  3. Compare fictional pupil responses, separating what is visible in the work from inferred explanations.
  4. Draft an inquiry question and a short teacher think-aloud illustrating planning, monitoring and evaluation.

Required reading

OL1 reflection selection; E1; E2.

Formative check

Classify statements as observation, inference or hypothesis; compare the think-aloud with an annotated exemplar. Feedback challenges explanations that attribute difficulty to fixed pupil characteristics without evidence.

Conceptual advance

From impressions to an explicit learning question.

Your output

A one-page inquiry brief identifying the learning objective, observed difficulty, initial explanation, alternative explanation and evidence needed.

Connection forward: the brief identifies the mathematical thinking that Module 2 must make visible.

02Make mathematical thinking visibleWhat does a representation reveal or conceal?5 hours

Learning outcomes

Distinguish additive from multiplicative relationships; connect verbal, visual, tabular and symbolic representations; diagnose a plausible misconception; and design questions that elicit reasoning.

Self-paced activities

  1. Compare a proportional situation with a superficially similar situation requiring additive reasoning.
  2. Represent a relationship using a diagram or manipulatives, a ratio table and a word formula.
  3. Use Polypad or Desmos to test a prediction, including a case in which a proposed rule fails.
  4. Analyse two fictional pupil solutions and identify what each pupil appears to understand.
  5. Design a 15–20-minute teaching sequence containing a worked example, a contrasting example, a think-aloud and an independent check.

Required reading

OL2 modelling-cycle selection; OL3 relationships selection; OL4 proportional-reasoning selections; E3.

Formative check

Diagnose a new pupil response and explain why one representation is more informative than another. Correct calculation alone is insufficient.

Conceptual advance

From answers to relationships, explanations and misconceptions.

Tools: Polypad or Desmos, with paper alternatives.

Your output

The annotated task, anticipated responses, diagnostic questions and teaching sequence.

Connection forward: participants identify what evidence would show improved reasoning; those indicators become variables in Module 3.

03Investigate evidence and uncertaintyWhat can these data support?5 hours

Learning outcomes

Formulate a statistical question; justify a summary and graphical representation; distinguish percentage change from percentage-point change; and identify limitations arising from measurement and sampling.

Self-paced activities

  1. Apply the pose–collect–analyse–interpret (PCAI) cycle to the common case.
  2. Inspect a synthetic dataset containing missing values, uneven participation and variation in pupil performance.
  3. Use CODAP to compare distributions and examine individual cases.
  4. Check how the interpretation changes when an unusual value is included, a different average is chosen or the denominator changes.
  5. Write a conclusion that separates observed change from an explanation of its cause.

Required reading

OL2 percentage points; OL3 averages and spread; OL4 PCAI cycle; OL5 measurement and sampling selections.

Formative check

Identify an unsupported claim in a short report and repair it. Explain why a before-and-after increase does not, by itself, establish that the teaching strategy caused the change.

Conceptual advance

From numerical summaries to warranted interpretations.

Tool: CODAP, with supplied tables and plots.

Your output

A compact data investigation with one appropriate graph, justified summaries, a qualified conclusion and an improved collection plan.

Connection forward: unresolved explanations—attendance, prior knowledge, task differences or access—become inputs to the systems investigation.

04Understand the classroom systemWhat else could influence the outcome?5 hours

Learning outcomes

Distinguish systemic from systematic thinking; define and justify a system boundary; compare stakeholder perspectives; and propose an intervention while acknowledging possible unintended consequences.

Self-paced activities

  1. Re-examine the initial explanation of pupils’ difficulty.
  2. Draw a paper map showing relevant people, practices, resources and relationships.
  3. Compare supplied pupil, teacher and school-leader perspectives; record whose perspective is still missing.
  4. Use a small LOOPY model to explore a proposed feedback relationship, such as confidence, participation and opportunities to receive feedback.
  5. Change the model boundary or one relationship and reconsider the intervention.

Required reading

OL6 selections on difficulties and messes, systemic practice, boundaries and multiple perspectives.

Formative check

Explain the difference between a relationship supported by evidence and one assumed for exploration. Identify an omitted influence and explain whether including it changes the decision.

Conceptual advance

From isolated explanations to boundaries, interactions and perspectives.

Tool: LOOPY, with a paper causal-map alternative.

Your output

A labelled systems map, boundary rationale, stakeholder comparison and revised intervention hypothesis.

Connection forward: the map supplies conditions and counterarguments that the decision in Module 5 must address.

LOOPY is an exploratory extension. Its behaviour is not presented as a validated prediction of classroom outcomes.

05Evaluate digital claims and justify a choiceWhich decision is best supported?5 hours

Learning outcomes

Investigate a source’s provenance; distinguish independent corroboration from repetition; map supporting and opposing reasons; and justify a digital-tool choice against a learning purpose.

Self-paced activities

  1. Evaluate competing claims about approaches to improving mathematical reasoning.
  2. Practise lateral reading: leave the original page, investigate its source and trace a claim towards its underlying evidence.
  3. Compare OpenLearn’s PROMPT checklist with lateral reading, recognising that polished presentation is not sufficient evidence of reliability.
  4. Build an argument map containing a main claim, supporting reasons, a serious objection, evidence and an explicit assumption.
  5. Compare an appropriate digital tool with a paper-based alternative using learning value, accessibility, practical demands and data handling.

Required reading

OL7 information-evaluation and argument-mapping selections; E4.

Formative check

Detect circular corroboration, a weak evidential link and an objection that has not been answered.

Conceptual advance

From collecting information to evaluating an argument.

Optional tool: Kialo Edu. A text argument outline fully meets the outcome.

Your output

A source audit, argument map and approximately 400-word decision brief stating the chosen approach, its conditions and what would change the decision.

Connection forward: the decision becomes a testable inquiry plan, rather than a recommendation with an assumed benefit.

06Design and evaluate a professional inquiryHow will I know whether to continue, adapt or stop?6 hours

Learning outcomes

Integrate learning theory, mathematical reasoning, data interpretation and systems analysis; design a feasible evaluation; and revise a proposal in response to a structured critique.

Self-paced activities

  1. Assemble and revise the outputs from Modules 1–5.
  2. Specify a short instructional sequence and a credible mechanism through which it might improve reasoning.
  3. Design comparable initial and follow-up tasks and an explanation-quality rubric.
  4. Rehearse the analysis using supplied synthetic responses.
  5. Apply the capstone rubric and a provided critical-review checklist.
  6. Record revisions and establish criteria for continuing, adapting or stopping the approach.

Required reading

OL1 action-planning selection; targeted revisiting of earlier readings.

Formative check

A readiness audit examines alignment between the question, teaching activity, evidence, analysis and decision criteria.

Conceptual advance

From a justified proposal to an evaluable plan.

Your output

The final professional inquiry portfolio, assembled largely from your existing module outputs.

What the sources support—and their boundaries
  • The adult-learning material supports reflection and planning; it is not treated as a comprehensive account of adolescent learning. Personal learning preferences are not converted into fixed “learning-style” labels.
  • The mathematics self-study sources support mathematical understanding; pedagogical claims are drawn principally from Teaching mathematics and external research guidance.
  • The supplied Teaching mathematics PDF ends during Week 8. Later sections mentioned within it are not assumed to be available.
  • The systems PDF explicitly omits some audiovisual instruction. Required activities therefore need original, self-contained diagram guidance; the missing media remain optional.
  • The digital-tools source supports reasoning and evaluation. Its historical software and AI discussions are not used as current product guidance or as evidence about contemporary generative AI.

06 / The professional inquiry

Bring the thinking together.
Make the next decision better.

Develop and justify a small-scale inquiry into improving middle-school pupils’ mathematical reasoning. Explain the learning difficulty, design an instructional response, examine contextual influences, evaluate relevant claims and specify how evidence would inform the next teaching decision.

Final submission: approximately 1,500–2,000 words plus selected artefacts, assembled largely from existing module outputs.

01

Inquiry brief

Context, curriculum objective, focused question and initial evidence.

02

Reasoning task & teaching sequence

Representations, anticipated misconceptions, explicit modelling and an independent check.

03

Evidence plan & worked analysis

Variables, collection method, appropriate summaries and a rehearsal using the supplied dataset.

04

Systems analysis

Boundary, relevant perspectives, possible interactions and unintended consequences.

05

Decision justification

Argument map, source audit, digital-tool rationale and alternative approach.

06

Evaluation & revision plan

Comparable tasks, success indicators, limitations, practical timetable and criteria for changing course.

An example inquiry question

What changes in pupils’ explanations of proportional relationships are visible after a short sequence combining contrasting examples, explicit think-alouds and linked representations?

The inquiry assesses explanatory quality as well as accuracy. Participants must distinguish simulated findings, existing classroom evidence and proposed future collection.

Completion within 30 hours does not depend on demonstrating an actual pupil improvement. The assessed outcome is a coherent, evidence-informed, feasible inquiry with a defensible interpretation of the rehearsal evidence. A later classroom trial may extend the portfolio.

07 / Assessment

Judgement, supported
by evidence.

Assessment is criterion-referenced and developmental.

  • Entry diagnostic: ungraded; informs optional support.
  • Module checks: short application questions with explanations, worked responses and retry opportunities.
  • Portfolio development: each module includes self-assessment against a small set of criteria.
  • Capstone: assessed through the common rubric below.

Proposed achievement rule

At least 70% overall, with the mathematical-accuracy and evidence-interpretation criteria each judged satisfactory. Material mathematical errors or unsupported causal conclusions require revision.

For a fully independent course, participants use annotated exemplars and the rubric, and completion is self-attested. If the provider wants to certify demonstrated professional competence, asynchronous human assessment of the capstone is needed; that assessor workload is separate from participants’ 30 hours.

No Open University badge, endorsement or accreditation is implied.

09 / Responsible reuse

Credit the sources.
Respect the permissions.

The proposed course should use a link-first reading model, with original connecting explanations, fictional cases, datasets and assessment materials.

Copyright, licensing and attribution considerations
  • OpenLearn: the supplied PDFs generally specify CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, subject to exclusions and third-party rights. Adaptations require attribution, identification of changes and compliance with the applicable non-commercial and share-alike conditions. Check acknowledgements before reproducing images or media. OpenLearn reuse guidance.
  • OpenStax: the current Contemporary Mathematics pages identify an Attribution–NonCommercial–ShareAlike licence. Record the licence of the precise edition used rather than assuming all OpenStax editions have identical terms. Book attribution and licence.
  • EEF: its stated terms permit copying for non-commercial research and educational purposes with acknowledgement and copyright notices, without modification. Link to the report or use permitted unchanged extracts; do not assume permission to adapt its graphics. EEF terms.
  • National Academies: free online reading and PDF availability do not establish permission to adapt or republish the book. Link to the required sections and check permissions for reproduction. Official book reader.
  • PhET: the current licensing information identifies regular HTML simulations as CC BY-NC 4.0; confirm the specific simulation/version before redistribution. PhET licensing.
  • Other resources and tools: free access, software licensing, hosted-service terms, user-contributed content and dataset rights are distinct. Check the particular NRICH task, COR resource, GeoGebra construction or Gapminder dataset before embedding or adapting it.

Maintain a rights register recording title, creator, version, URL, licence, permitted use, third-party exclusions and changes made. A commercial delivery model would require a specific rights review before republishing non-commercially licensed material.

This six-module architecture, the common proportional-reasoning inquiry, the required source selections, the limited digital-tool pathway, the portfolio assessment and the 30-hour allocation.
